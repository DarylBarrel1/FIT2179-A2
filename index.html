Part 02 — Southeast Asia
A region of stark contrasts in healthcare and longevity

Southeast Asia is one of the most diverse regions in the world, not just culturally, but in how its people access healthcare. From Singapore's world-class hospitals to the Philippines' stretched public system, the gap between the best and worst resourced countries is stark. Understanding these differences is the first step to understanding why life expectancy varies so dramatically across the region.

Graph 4 — Hospital beds per 1,000 people across Southeast Asia and Australia Our World in Data / WHO
Graph 5 — Hospital beds by country (latest available) Our World in Data / WHO
Graph 6 — Life expectancy overtime Our World in Data / WHO

The relationship between public health spending and life expectancy is telling but it is not simple. Countries that dedicate a higher share of their budget to healthcare generally see longer lives, but Singapore is a notable exception: it spends less publicly than Australia yet achieves comparable life expectancy. This suggests that how a country spends its healthcare budget and how efficiently its system delivers care matters just as much as how much it spends.

Graph 7 — Physicians per 1,000 by country Our World in Data / WHO
Graph 8 — Life expectancy vs Public Health Expenditure Our World in Data / WHO

Across Southeast Asia, the story of healthcare is one of gradual but uneven progress. Every country in the region has improved its life expectancy since 1970, a testament to decades of investment in better nutrition, sanitation, vaccination and medical access. Yet the gains have not been equal. Singapore has pulled far ahead, building a healthcare system that rivals the best in the world. Meanwhile, countries like the Philippines and Indonesia with populations in the tens of millions spread across thousands of islands continue to struggle with critical shortages of doctors, hospitals and funding. With fewer than two physicians per 1,000 people, millions of patients in these countries face long distances, long waits and limited options when they fall ill. The challenge for Southeast Asia is not just economic, it is structural. Building the infrastructure, training the workforce and funding the systems needed to close these gaps will take decades of sustained commitment.

Part 03 — The Connection
Does investing more in healthcare actually save lives?
Graph 9 — Health spending vs life expectancy (bubble size = physicians per 1,000) Our World in Data / WHO
Graph 10 — Life expectancy ranking across the region (2022) Our World in Data / WHO

The bubble chart makes it clear that countries with more doctors per person consistently achieve higher life expectancy. Australia and Singapore sit at the top which contrasts Philippines, where it is located on the opposing end with fewer physicians and lower spending and the shortest lives in the region. Additionally, the dot plot confirms the ranking, reinforcing our claim over the bubble chart.

Graph 11 — Life expectancy change from 2000 to 2022 Our World in Data / WHO
Graph 12 — Hospital Beds per 1,000 People vs Life Expectancy Our World in Data / WHO

The data tells a clear story: countries that invest more in healthcare — both in infrastructure and workforce — tend to have longer-living populations. But the relationship is not perfectly linear. Singapore demonstrates that efficiency and quality of care matter as much as raw spending. Australia leads the region not just because it spends more, but because it has built a system that delivers care equitably across a vast geography.

For Southeast Asia, the challenge is not simply spending more — it is spending better, and ensuring that investment reaches the communities that need it most.
